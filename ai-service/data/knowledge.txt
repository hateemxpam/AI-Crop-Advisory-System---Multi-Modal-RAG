Wheat rust appears as yellow, brown, or black powder on leaves and stems.
The disease spreads quickly in cool and humid weather, especially in dense fields.
Use resistant seed varieties, avoid late sowing, and spray a recommended fungicide early.

Loose smut in wheat shows black dusty heads instead of healthy grains at flowering.
The fungus lives inside seed, so infected seed can spread the problem next season.
Use certified treated seed and apply proper seed treatment before sowing.

Powdery mildew in wheat looks like white powder patches on leaves and leaf sheaths.
It reduces leaf photosynthesis, which lowers grain filling and final yield.
Maintain balanced fertilizer use and spray fungicide when early symptoms appear.

Root rot in wheat causes weak plants, yellow leaves, and poor tiller growth.
Waterlogging and continuous wheat cropping can increase this soil-borne disease.
Improve drainage, rotate crops, and use disease-free seed to reduce losses.

Rice blast causes spindle-shaped spots with gray centers on leaves and necks.
It is severe in cloudy weather, high humidity, and excess nitrogen conditions.
Use resistant varieties, split nitrogen doses, and spray fungicide at early signs.

Bacterial leaf blight in rice starts with yellowing at leaf tips and margins.
The disease spreads through rain splash, irrigation water, and infected plant material.
Use clean seed, avoid field injury, and follow recommended copper-based sprays if needed.

Sheath blight in rice forms oval lesions on leaf sheaths near the water line.
Dense planting and high nitrogen create a humid canopy that favors infection.
Keep proper plant spacing, manage nitrogen carefully, and use fungicide at booting stage.

Brown spot in rice produces small brown lesions and can reduce grain quality.
It is common in nutrient-poor soils, especially where potash and silicon are low.
Apply balanced fertilizer, improve organic matter, and avoid moisture stress during growth.

Maize leaf blight creates long gray-brown lesions that spread along leaves.
The disease can dry leaves early and reduce cob development in severe cases.
Use resistant hybrids, remove infected residues, and spray fungicide when needed.

Downy mildew in maize causes pale stripes and stunted growth in young plants.
Infected plants may not form normal cobs and can become sterile.
Use treated seed, rogue infected plants early, and rotate maize with non-host crops.

Stalk rot in maize weakens stems and causes plants to lodge before harvest.
Poor drainage, nutrient stress, and insect injury can increase stalk rot incidence.
Maintain balanced nutrition, avoid water stress, and harvest on time to reduce damage.

Maize mosaic virus causes mottled yellow-green leaves and poor plant vigor.
The virus is often spread by insect vectors such as aphids and leafhoppers.
Control vectors early, remove infected plants, and use tolerant hybrids where available.

Nitrogen fertilizer helps crops grow green leaves and strong stems quickly.
Too much nitrogen can cause soft growth, pest attack, and disease pressure.
Apply in split doses based on crop stage instead of giving all at once.

Phosphorus supports root development and early crop establishment after sowing.
Poor phosphorus supply can lead to stunted plants with dark green or purplish leaves.
Place phosphorus near the root zone at planting for better uptake.

Potassium improves disease tolerance, water balance, and grain quality in crops.
Deficient plants may show leaf edge burning and weak stress tolerance.
Apply muriate of potash at recommended rates after soil test guidance.

Urea is a common nitrogen source and should be used with moisture in soil.
If urea stays on dry soil surface, some nitrogen can be lost to the air.
Apply before irrigation or light rain and avoid use during very hot midday periods.

DAP provides both nitrogen and phosphorus for early crop growth.
It is useful at sowing time when roots are developing rapidly.
Do not overuse DAP alone; combine with potash and other nutrients as needed.

Zinc deficiency is common in rice and maize soils with high pH.
Plants may show yellow striping, short internodes, and poor tillering.
Use zinc sulfate as soil application or foliar spray according to recommendation.

Sulfur helps protein formation and improves grain quality in cereals.
Deficiency appears as overall yellowing, often first on younger leaves.
Apply gypsum or sulfur-containing fertilizers where sulfur levels are low.

Micronutrients like boron, iron, and manganese are needed in small amounts.
Even small shortages can reduce flowering, grain setting, and crop health.
Use soil test based micronutrient blends instead of random mixed sprays.

Compost adds slow-release nutrients and improves soil structure over time.
It supports beneficial microbes that help nutrient cycling and root health.
Use well-decomposed compost before sowing and mix it evenly in soil.

Integrated nutrient management combines chemical fertilizer with organic inputs.
This approach improves yield stability and reduces long-term soil degradation.
Plan fertilizer schedule by crop stage, soil test, and local advisory.

First irrigation in wheat is critical around crown root initiation stage.
Late first irrigation can reduce tillers and lower final grain yield.
Irrigate on time and avoid standing water for long periods in wheat fields.

Rice fields need water management, not permanent deep flooding all season.
Continuous deep water wastes water and may reduce root oxygen.
Use alternate wetting and drying where suitable to save water and maintain yield.

Maize is sensitive to moisture stress at tasseling and silking stages.
Water shortage at these stages can sharply reduce kernel formation.
Prioritize irrigation during flowering and grain filling for better cobs.

Laser land leveling improves uniform water distribution across the field.
It reduces waterlogging in low spots and dryness in high spots.
This practice saves irrigation water and supports uniform crop growth.

Irrigate early morning or late evening to reduce evaporation losses.
Hot daytime irrigation can waste water and create plant stress.
Use proper field channels to deliver water evenly to all rows.

Drip irrigation gives water directly near root zones in row crops.
It reduces weed growth between rows and improves fertilizer efficiency.
Regularly clean filters and check emitters to keep the system working well.

Sprinkler irrigation is useful in sandy soils and uneven fields.
Strong wind can reduce uniformity, so timing and nozzle selection matter.
Avoid over-sprinkling to prevent leaf diseases and nutrient leaching.

Do not irrigate by fixed calendar only; check soil moisture first.
Over-irrigation causes nutrient loss, root disease, and poor aeration.
Use a soil probe or hand feel method to decide irrigation need.

Good drainage is as important as irrigation in heavy soils.
Standing water for long periods can damage roots and increase fungal diseases.
Maintain field drains and remove blockages before rainy periods.

Mulching with crop residue reduces evaporation from the soil surface.
It helps maintain cooler soil temperature and suppresses weed growth.
Use clean residue mulch in row crops without touching plant stems.

Fall armyworm in maize feeds on whorl leaves and makes ragged holes.
Early scouting is important because young larvae are easier to control.
Use pheromone traps, hand destruction in small fields, and recommended insecticides.

Stem borer in rice and maize causes dead hearts and white heads.
Larvae live inside stems, making late sprays less effective.
Monitor moth activity and apply control at egg or early larval stage.

Aphids suck plant sap and can spread viral diseases quickly.
Heavy infestation causes leaf curling, yellowing, and sticky honeydew.
Encourage natural enemies and spray selective insecticide only when threshold is crossed.

Brown planthopper in rice can cause hopper burn and sudden drying patches.
Excess nitrogen and dense planting can increase pest outbreaks.
Avoid over-fertilization and use need-based insecticide with rotation of mode of action.

Whitefly attacks many crops and transmits plant viruses.
Warm weather and repeated broad-spectrum sprays can worsen whitefly problems.
Use yellow sticky traps, field sanitation, and targeted sprays when necessary.

Termites damage roots and stems in dry soil conditions.
Weak plants and undecomposed residues can attract termite attack.
Treat seed where recommended and maintain proper soil moisture.

Cutworms cut young seedlings at ground level during night feeding.
Damage is often patchy and severe in newly planted fields.
Use light traps, deep plowing before sowing, and bait application if needed.

Red flour beetle and storage pests damage grains after harvest.
Poor drying and damp storage increase insect multiplication.
Dry grain to safe moisture and store in clean, airtight structures.

Rodents in fields and storage cause direct loss and contamination.
They also damage bunds, irrigation channels, and stored grain bags.
Use burrow management, trap methods, and community-level control programs.

Integrated pest management combines monitoring, prevention, and safe control methods.
It reduces unnecessary pesticide use and protects beneficial insects.
Spray only at economic threshold level with correct dose and timing.

Soil organic matter improves water holding, nutrient supply, and root growth.
Low organic matter soils become hard, less fertile, and prone to erosion.
Add farmyard manure, compost, and crop residues regularly.

Crop rotation breaks pest cycles and improves soil nutrient balance.
Growing the same crop repeatedly can increase disease and nutrient depletion.
Rotate cereals with legumes to improve nitrogen and soil structure.

Soil testing shows nutrient status and helps correct fertilizer planning.
Without testing, farmers may overuse one nutrient and miss another.
Test soil every two to three years and follow local recommendations.

Green manuring with legume crops adds biomass and natural nitrogen.
Incorporated green plants improve soil microbial activity and tilth.
Use green manure before main crop in fields with poor fertility.

Soil pH affects nutrient availability and fertilizer performance.
Very alkaline or very acidic soils can lock important nutrients.
Use amendments like gypsum or lime based on soil test advice.

Salinity in soil causes poor germination and stunted plant growth.
White crust on soil surface is a common visible sign.
Improve drainage, use good quality irrigation water, and grow tolerant varieties.

Avoid burning crop residues because it destroys soil life and organic carbon.
Burning also causes air pollution and nutrient loss from the field.
Incorporate residues or compost them for long-term soil health.

Conservation tillage reduces soil disturbance and protects topsoil.
It helps reduce erosion and keeps moisture in dry periods.
Combine reduced tillage with residue cover for better soil resilience.
